## Working title vs. public framing

Internally this stays "the B4/B6 siting-mismatch study" for continuity with every cross-reference in this project's evidence log. But if this is ever put in front of anyone outside this project, it should be framed as a question, not a finding: **"Where would additional flexible electricity demand in Scotland do the most to reduce renewable curtailment, and does the current AI/data-centre development pipeline overlap with those locations?"** That framing survives scrutiny; "AI data centres are being sited in the wrong place" does not yet, on the evidence below.

## The question, in plain terms

Scotland has more wind power than its grid can move south at some hours. When that happens, wind farms get paid to switch off ("curtailed") — a cost ultimately passed to consumers. AI data centres are one of the few loads big enough and (for some workloads) flexible enough to soak up that otherwise-wasted power *if* they're built in the right place. Developers are currently choosing where to build based on their own commercial logic — land, substations, planning, labour. Nobody had checked whether those two things — where developers want to build, and where the electricity system would most benefit from a large flexible load — actually point at the same places. This study checks.

*Going deeper:* B4 is not a precise ranking of "value per site" — it is a screening boundary. It tells you which side of a known transmission pinch-point a project sits on, not its exact marginal value to the system, which would depend on local headroom, timing, and what else connects nearby. Everything below uses B4 in that limited sense: a real, binding constraint that is a reasonable first-cut proxy for "north = more curtailment-adjacent," not a complete locational marginal-value calculation.

## Primary hypothesis (revised)

Scotland's commercial AI/data-centre development pipeline shows a strong geographic pattern — heavily concentrated south of the B4 boundary — that tracks conventional commercial and infrastructure criteria rather than proximity to the transmission constraint associated with Scotland's most curtailed wind generation. This is evidence consistent with the pipeline not being drawn toward locations of stronger potential value for absorbing north-to-south renewable-energy constraint, though it does not by itself establish that those locations offer the *greatest* system value — that would require a full locational value calculation this study has not performed.

*(This replaces the earlier wording, which asserted the pipeline was sited away from "the locations where additional flexible demand would provide the greatest value" — a claim this study was never positioned to prove, since it conflates three separable things: (a) where the pipeline actually is, (b) where curtailment is worst, and (c) where a flexible load would provide the greatest marginal system value. This study has strong evidence on (a) and reasonable evidence on (b); it has not attempted (c).)*

## Null hypothesis (revised)

H₀: The spatial distribution of proposed AI/data-centre capacity is adequately explained by conventional commercial and infrastructure siting factors (land availability, substation access, planning history, workforce, fibre), without needing to invoke any systematic avoidance of, or indifference to, constraint-relevant locations.

*(The honest limitation here, unchanged in substance from before but now stated plainly: this study has not built a complete "commercially plausible site" counterfactual — see the Tier 3 section below — so H₀ cannot yet be rejected on distributional grounds alone. What the evidence below does is narrow the space of explanations for why H₀ might still be true, not disprove it outright. The most rigorous single piece of Tier 3 evidence obtained so far — a full quantitative analysis of Scotland's Vacant and Derelict Land Survey, T3-08 below — points substantially, but not completely, toward H₀.)*

## Reframed structure: three tiers, layered on the original three layers

The original Layer 1/2/3 build-out is kept below unchanged (it's the evidentiary record), but it's worth naming explicitly what kind of claim each layer actually supports, because the review correctly pointed out that blurring these together is where the earlier verdict overreached:

- **Tier 1 — spatial exposure.** Where the pipeline is, relative to B4/B6. This is the strongest, most complete part of the study (23/23 projects verified). It establishes *pattern*, not *cause* or *optimality*.
- **Tier 2 — connection feasibility.** Whether large flexible demand connections were actually available and being taken up north of B4 by anyone. This is well-evidenced via SSEN Transmission's own registers (Green Volt/New Deer and others) and closes off the "no such connection existed" counter-explanation.
- **Tier 3 — commercial counterfactual.** What fraction of Scotland's commercially plausible large-data-centre sites (by brownfield/industrial land, grid connectivity, fibre, workforce, transport, planning allocation) lie north vs south of B4, independent of what's actually been proposed. **This tier is now substantially, though not completely, built out (see `evidence-log.md`, Thread T3), and the evidence cuts in multiple directions:**
  - *Toward the primary hypothesis:* Highland, Aberdeen City, Aberdeenshire and Moray Councils publicly organised a joint bid for AI Growth Zone-type status covering nearly the whole north-of-B4 area, and at least one industry publication (Techerati) independently describes the Highland area as "AI-ready" on land/grid/renewables grounds — yet it lost out nationally to Lanarkshire, and zero AI data-centre projects in the Layer-1 pipeline are sited there. Unverified secondary sourcing (council social-media posts not yet read in full; trade press, not a primary land/grid source) — a Freedom of Information request to DSIT was drafted the same evening, not yet sent.
  - *Toward the null hypothesis, now precisely quantified (T3-08):* a full council-by-council analysis of the Scottish Government's Vacant and Derelict Land Survey 2025 register (3,091 sites, 8,860.7 hectares nationally, obtained directly from Andy's own downloaded copy after this session's network access to gov.scot was blocked) shows previously-developed vacant/derelict land is heavily concentrated south of B4: **318 sites (10.3%) / 1,251.6 hectares (14.1%) north of B4, vs 2,688 sites (87.0%) / 7,503.3 hectares (84.7%) south**, with a further 85 sites (2.7%) / 105.8 hectares (1.2%) in the ambiguous Argyll and Bute / Perth and Kinross fringe that this study excludes rather than resolves. North Lanarkshire alone holds 1,228.1 hectares across 413 sites — nearly as much as the entire north-of-B4 total. Restricting to the large parcels (≥20 hectares) that most data-centre developments actually need, the split moves only slightly: 8 northern sites totalling 819.8ha vs 65 southern sites totalling 3,468.2ha, roughly 81%/19% south/north by hectares among large parcels — a marginally *higher* northern share than the 84.7%/14.1% split across all sites, not a lower one. **The synthesis this produces: land availability alone would predict something like an 81-87% southern share of a hypothetical brownfield-only pipeline, not the 91-96% southern share actually observed in Layer 1. The residual gap is real but modest — roughly 5 to 10 percentage points — and sits close to the edge of what a 23-project, single-large-project-swings-the-percentage dataset can distinguish from noise.** Three caveats apply throughout: (1) the SVDLS only covers previously-developed vacant/derelict land, not the farmland that most of the actual pipeline sits on, so this is a partial proxy for "commercially plausible sites," not a complete one; (2) the ambiguous Argyll and Bute / Perth and Kinross councils are excluded from the clean split rather than resolved, for lack of a precise B4 topology source; (3) with only 23 pipeline projects, a 5-10 percentage-point gap is at the edge of statistical distinguishability from sampling noise.
  - *Narrowing, but not resolving, a third explanation:* a fuller read of SSEN Transmission's own substation inventory shows a dense network of existing 132-400kV grid nodes across the far north, well beyond the eight hubs already studied — weakening, without eliminating, any "there's nowhere to plug in up there" account.
  - *A fourth, deliberately descriptive addition, not folded into any percentage (T3-09):* at Andy's suggestion, grouse-moor/shooting-estate land was checked as its own category, separate from both brownfield and ordinary farmland, without taking a position on the underlying ecological debate. A Scottish Government-commissioned GIS study (SEFARI/James Hutton Institute) puts grouse-moor-managed land at roughly 1 million hectares nationally (858,000ha rough grazing, 163,000ha actively burned as of 2018). Its own named areas of highest management intensity — the Cairngorms, Angus Glens, and Lammermuir Hills, verified by direct quotation from the source report — do not sit cleanly on one side of B4: the Angus Glens and Lammermuirs are south, the Cairngorms straddle the boundary. A fifth area the same report names, the Pentlands/Moorfoot Hills (also south of B4), shows signs of *ceased* management. No north/south hectare split has been built for this category, and the source data may not support as clean a split as the brownfield-land analysis did even if pursued further.
  - *A methodological caveat surfaced by checking Andy's own challenge to the study's framing (T3-10):* the "brownfield vs farmland" binary this study has used throughout Tier 3 rests on a specific legal-planning test — whether a permanent structure ever stood on the land — which has nothing to do with land-management intensity or ecological condition. Checked directly against the standard planning definition: brownfield/"previously developed land" excludes "land that is or was last occupied by agricultural or forestry buildings," and requires an actual structure, not just intensive use. Grouse-moor land generally has no such structure (butts and hill tracks don't qualify) and is continuously used, not vacant — so it is **invisible to both categories** this study has used: not brownfield (T3-06/T3-08's SVDLS dataset only covers vacant/derelict previously-developed land), and not "ordinary farmland" either. This is a genuine methodological gap, not just a framing question, and it generalises: Andy's separate observation that sheep grazing also materially alters the landscape describes the same underlying issue — "greenfield" as used throughout Tier 3 treats an intensively grazed hillside, a chemically-farmed arable field, and an untouched hillside as identical, because the planning test only asks about past buildings, not present land-use intensity.
  - *A fifth addition, checking the size of "large" itself (T3-12, added 20 September 2026):* the ≥20 hectare threshold T3-08 used as a proxy for "single-parcel, data-centre-campus scale" turns out to be well below what a real AI data-centre campus actually needs. Cross-checked against current reporting: xAI's Colossus 1 (Memphis) sits on ~39ha; OpenAI's Stargate (Abilene, full build) ~350ha; Meta's Holly Ridge (Louisiana) ~1,100ha; general modern hyperscale campuses commonly run 80-200ha, with some exceeding 320-400ha. Most importantly, **Hunterston — the one live Scottish hyperscale-scale proposal in this study's own Layer-1 table — sits on an 88.16-hectare site**, roughly 4.4 times T3-08's ≥20ha cut. This means the 8 north / 65 south "large-parcel" split in T3-08 may not represent parcels actually large enough to host a genuine AI data-centre campus on either side of B4 — it is a reasonable "large for a derelict-land inventory" threshold, not a "large enough for this specific use" threshold. A re-cut of the SVDLS register at a higher, more realistic threshold (60-90ha+) has not yet been attempted. A related, separate point worth holding onto rather than merging in: Hunterston itself sits on Grade 3 agricultural (greenfield) land, not brownfield — the one concrete example of the scale this study cares about didn't use brownfield land at all, which raises the further, still-untested possibility that large contiguous brownfield parcels may not exist in Scotland at true hyperscale scale on either side of B4, in which case the farmland-vs-brownfield "choice" the rest of Tier 3 treats as revealing developer preference may not be a free choice at that scale at all.

  - *A sixth addition, closing the largest remaining Tier 3 gap to a first approximation (T3-13, added 20 September 2026):* the farmland land-availability counterfactual — what T3-08 did for brownfield land, applied to ordinary farmland, where most of the actual pipeline sits — was checked using the Scottish Government's own "Economic Report on Scottish Agriculture, 2016" (June 2015 agricultural-census data by pre-1996 sub-region, the closest primary-source equivalent to a farmland register this study could find). **The result cuts the opposite way from T3-08: total Utilised Agricultural Area is majority NORTH of B4 — 61.3% (2,740,392ha) north vs 38.7% (1,731,474ha) south, on a clean basis excluding the ambiguous Argyll & Bute and Tayside/Perth-and-Kinross sub-regions.** Most of that northern total is Highland's own enormous area, and the same source states in its own words that Highland has "by far the largest share of grass and rough grazing," while "Grampian and Tayside had the largest share of crop and fallow land" — so the north's abundance is concentrated in extensive hill grazing, not higher-quality arable-capable land. A supplementary breakdown of crop-and-fallow land alone puts the split much closer to even (roughly 50.5% north / 49.5% south), though this narrower figure carries an unresolved internal reconciliation gap and should be read as directionally suggestive rather than as solid as the total-UAA figure. Either way, farmland availability explains markedly *less* of the pipeline's 91-96% southern concentration than brownfield availability did — the reverse conclusion from T3-08, now covering the majority (farmland) rather than the minority (brownfield-reuse) part of the pipeline. Independent corroboration: every one of the pipeline's own farmland sites (Coldstream, Ochiltree, Duns, Haspielaw, Auchtertool, Dunbar) sits in the crop-and-fallow-richer south, not in Highland or Grampian's much larger raw hectare totals — consistent with developers selecting for land *quality*, not raw *quantity*. Caveats: this is 2015 data (no more current equivalent found), uses coarser pre-1996 sub-regions rather than local authorities, excludes a much larger "ambiguous" share (~20% of the national total, versus T3-08's 1.2%) because Tayside couldn't be cleanly separated from Perth and Kinross, and — like T3-08's brownfield count — captures land-use category only, not flatness, accessibility, or grid proximity.

  This remains a genuinely open conceptual gap, but it is now addressed to a first approximation rather than entirely missing: T3-08 (brownfield) and T3-13 (farmland) together show that the two halves of the pipeline sit on very different land-availability stories — the brownfield-reuse minority is roughly where the brownfield land is; the farmland-sited majority is considerably more concentrated south than the farmland base alone would predict. A genuinely current, local-authority-level, buildability-filtered version of the farmland analysis (mirroring T3-08's rigor) has not been attempted, nor has a re-cut of the brownfield-land data at a realistic hyperscale-parcel size (T3-12).

## Layer 2 — the electricity-system spatial score

Anchored on NESO's own [ETYS "Scottish boundaries"](https://www.neso.energy/publications/electricity-ten-year-statement-etys/electricity-transmission-network-requirements/scottish-boundaries) page.

- **B4**: boundary between SSEN Transmission (north/central Highlands, Aberdeenshire, Moray, the isles) and SP Transmission (Central Belt and southern Scotland). 4GW capability, limited by the Tealing–Westfield 275kV circuit. Used throughout this study as a screening boundary, not a proxy for a complete locational marginal-value calculation (see above).
- **B6**: boundary between SP Transmission and NGET — the Scotland–England border itself. 6.7GW capability, limited by Harker–Moffat 400kV.
- Modo Energy (independent energy-market analyst) states, in its own words: B4 was **"constrained 42%"** of the time in 2025, B6 **"34%"**; and **"wind in North Scotland accounts for 60-62% of total system-bid volume."** *Caveat, added after independent re-verification (see `evidence-log.md`, RR-11): Modo's publicly accessible page does not state, in the text available without the full paywalled report, exactly what "constrained 42%" is a percentage of, or whether "system-bid volume" is equivalent to a share of Scotland's wind generation or capacity. This study previously paraphrased the second figure as "60%+ of Scotland's wind" — that paraphrase was an unverified translation and has been removed. Only Modo's exact wording is used here, with the denominator question left explicitly open rather than assumed.* The three most-curtailed assets in 2025 (Seagreen 2.7 TWh, Moray East 1.7 TWh, Moray West 1.4 TWh) all connect in on the north side — this figure, unlike the bid-volume one, is a direct energy quantity and not subject to the same denominator ambiguity. Total 2025 cost of managing these constraints: ~£350m in curtailment payments alone, over £1bn including replacement generation. Their exact connection points are now confirmed directly from SSEN Transmission's own registers: **Seagreen 1 at Tealing 275/33kV** (1,075MW, Built), **Moray East at New Deer 400kV** (900MW, Built), **Moray West at the Moray Firth offshore platform** (860MW, Built).

*A related, separately-sourced figure worth cross-referencing (see `evidence-log.md`, EC-06): trade-press reporting puts Scottish wind curtailment payments at £343m in 2025 (94% of the whole-GB total), with over 10TWh curtailed in Scotland and 8.8TWh of that in Northern Scotland specifically — only 61% of potential northern wind output reaching the grid. This is UNVERIFIED-SECONDARY (industry press, not yet traced to a primary NESO/Elexon figure) and roughly consistent with, rather than independently confirming, the ~£350m figure already used above from a different source.*

*Going deeper, on the balancing mechanism itself:* "wind farms get paid to switch off" is the plain-language version. Technically, this is the Balancing Mechanism's constraint management: when forecast generation on the north side of a binding boundary (like B4) would exceed what the boundary can carry, NESO issues a bid-offer instruction curtailing (turning down) the wind generator, and compensates it — typically at a price reflecting its lost revenue — while often also paying a generator on the other side of the constraint (frequently gas) to turn *up* and replace the lost output. The cost of both sides of that trade is socialised through Balancing Services Use of System (BSUoS) charges. A large, curtailable load sited on the *north* side of the boundary could in principle reduce curtailment volumes directly (by consuming power that would otherwise be constrained off) without needing a compensating turn-up elsewhere — which is the specific mechanism this study is testing whether the AI pipeline is positioned to exploit.

### A solid finding from SSEN Transmission's own connection registers: flexible demand IS being sought north of B4 — just not by AI compute

Reading SSEN Transmission's own TEC (Transmission Entry Capacity) Register and Embedded Register directly turned up something the study needed independently established. Several large "Demand"-tagged projects — not AI data centres, but green-hydrogen/electrolysis and battery-storage schemes — are actively queued for large flexible demand connections north of B4, including at **New Deer 400kV, the same substation Moray East connects at**: Green Volt Offshore Windfarm (two tranches, 560MW + 300MW, "Demand;Wind Offshore"). Also: CENOS/Flotation Energy — 1,350MW at Peterhead 400kV; Glenfiddich West/Boralex — 600MW; Cerulean Winds INTOG 1 — 600MW at Hurlie 400kV; plus several smaller BESS/PV/hydro demand co-locations.

This is arguably the single strongest piece of evidence in the whole study, because it pre-empts the most obvious counterargument: it isn't a developer's stated rationale, which might be self-serving or incomplete — it's what SSEN Transmission's own connection registers record, independent of developers' stated siting rationales. It weakens the explanation that large demand connections north of B4 are simply unavailable: SSEN's own registers show that very large demand projects are being pursued there, including at New Deer. AI compute simply isn't among them.

**Extending this the same evening:** a fuller read of SSEN Transmission's own substation inventory (its separate "Grid" and "Supergrid" open-data files, listing 132kV and 220-400kV sites respectively) confirms the north has substantial *existing* high-voltage infrastructure well beyond the eight hubs this study has focused on — at least 15 further 275-400kV substations across Caithness, Sutherland, Moray, and Aberdeenshire alone, and roughly two-thirds of SSEN Transmission's 132kV sites nationally sit in the same broad northern area. This is a grid-density finding, not a headroom or land finding, but it further narrows any "there's nowhere to plug in up there" account. One limitation: this project doesn't have a precise GIS boundary for B4 itself, only its two named endpoint substations (Tealing and Westfield), so a handful of SSEN Transmission sites in its own southern fringe (Perthshire/Argyll — Alyth, Tummel, Kinardochy, Inverarnan, Braco West, Crossaig) can't be confidently classified as north or south of B4 from coordinates alone.

### The headroom number the review actually asked for: attempted, and honestly still missing

A genuine £/MW-year or MW-headroom score (installed transformer capacity minus what's already contracted, substation by substation) was attempted twice from SSEN Transmission's own open data (the ETYS Transformers dataset, the substation-code index, and the Grid/Supergrid coordinate files), and both attempts are being reported honestly rather than papered over — this is the single most load-bearing missing number in the study, and it is treated as such:

- A national, automated join by name-matching failed for a structural reason, not just messy text: many register entries name a **generator's own private substation**, one step removed from the network-side node it actually connects into, so there's nothing to match them against in SSEN Transmission's asset lists. No reliable universal headroom map can be built from these files by name-matching alone.
- Narrowed to the eight major hubs this study actually cares about (Beauly, New Deer, Tealing, Blackhillock, Peterhead, Kintore, Keith, Fort Augustus), hand-checked figures show large committed-vs-queued gaps everywhere (e.g. Peterhead: 1,180MW committed vs 5,400MW total queued) — and **New Deer, the single node this study's argument depends on most, has no transformer/thermal rating recorded in SSEN Transmission's published Transformers dataset at all.**

**What this specific gap is, precisely, rather than vaguely:** the true remaining spare capacity at these nodes is not publicly stated. Two different things sit behind that absence, and they should not be conflated. A plain engineering fact — a substation's total installed transformer/thermal capacity — is very likely not commercially confidential (it isn't "the affairs of a particular business" in the sense Section 105 of the Utilities Act 2000 restricts, which is the same confidentiality regime this project has already had to navigate with UKPN); its absence from this particular CSV export looks more like an extraction gap than deliberate withholding, and is worth checking against NESO's full [ETYS documents and appendices](https://www.neso.energy/publications/electricity-ten-year-statement-etys/etys-documents-and-appendices) directly. By contrast, the *net* spare capacity once the live competing queue is taken into account genuinely does sit behind commercially sensitive, Section-105-type territory, because it reveals the competitive position of specific queued developers — and the honest, citable move there is to name that as a real, specific data gap rather than approximate around it.

**Outreach drafted 19 September 2026, not yet sent:** a narrowly scoped technical question to SSEN Transmission's connections team (transmission.commercial@sse.com), framed as genuine curiosity rather than a challenge — asking whether New Deer 400kV's installed transformer/thermal capacity is published anywhere beyond the opendatasoft export, and if not, whether SSEN can state it as a general engineering fact. A lighter, complementary note is also drafted for NESO's demand-forecasting team (ongoing correspondence), asking whether the gap is a known ETYS extraction issue and whether FES tracks co-located generation/demand connections like Green Volt's distinctly from pure generation. A third, separate Freedom of Information request to DSIT (drafted the same evening) pursues the AI Growth Zone bid question — see the Tier 3 section above. **A fourth clarifying question for NESO was drafted 20 September 2026** (see `evidence-log.md`, the energy-cost thread): whether NESO's FES/Ten Year Outlook modelling already factors in the November 2025 "Delivering AI Growth Zones" electricity-discount scheme when forecasting "little AI training taking place in the UK." All four are answerable in principle without touching anything commercially sensitive, and would each close a real data gap in the study — though even fully resolved answers to all of them would only complete Tiers 1 and 2 more precisely; none, by themselves, complete the still-open remainder of Tier 3 (the farmland counterfactual).

## Layer 3 — the comparison

### The straightforward version of the result

| | Project count | Share | Aggregate publicly stated capacity (MW) | Share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **North of B4** (SSEN Transmission area) | 1 (Blackdog) | 4% | 600 | 9% |
| **Between B4 and B6** (SP Transmission area) | 22 | 96% | ~6,240 | 91% |

*(Capacity split updated from ~600/6,040 to ~600/6,240 after Coldstream's capacity correction to 500MW — the percentages are effectively unchanged.)*

Against that: a large share of Scotland's wind (per Modo, 60-62% of total system-bid volume — see the denominator caveat above), and the three most-curtailed assets in the country, sit on the north side of a boundary that's binding 42% of the time — and only 9% of the identified data-centre capacity is sited there. This split is now against a **complete** Layer-1 pipeline (23 of 23 projects individually verified), not a partial one.

### The honest caveat — elevated, not buried

This split is essentially the crude "most curtailed wind nearby" proxy an earlier review warned against relying on alone, and that caveat deserves top billing rather than a footnote: **no local network-headroom data has been sourced** (see the New Deer gap above), **legitimate non-grid siting factors haven't been formally weighed** (thinner fibre outside the Central Belt, less flat regeneration land, smaller workforce), and — the largest remaining gap — **the commercial-site counterfactual (Tier 3) is now substantially, but not completely, built.** A 91%/9% capacity split is only evidence of a *mismatch* if a materially different split would have been achievable given where commercially viable sites actually exist. The most rigorous data point obtained so far on this question, T3-08's full council-by-council analysis of the Scottish Vacant and Derelict Land Survey, finds that previously-developed brownfield/derelict land alone would predict something like an 81-87% southern share — not the 91-96% actually observed. That leaves a real but modest residual gap of roughly 5-10 percentage points, at the edge of what a 23-project dataset can distinguish from noise, and it only speaks to the brownfield-reuse subset of the pipeline (Chapelcross, Cockenzie, Blackdog, Greenock, Drumshangie) rather than the farmland majority — for which a land-availability counterfactual now exists, to a first approximation, and points the opposite way (T3-13): Scotland's total farmland is majority north of B4 (61.3%/38.7%), so for the farmland-sited majority of the pipeline, land availability explains *less*, not more, of the observed southern concentration, though the north's farmland is concentrated in lower-quality rough grazing rather than the crop-and-fallow land the pipeline's own farmland sites actually use — and that farmland majority is itself not a uniform category (see T3-10 above): it spans a real intensity spectrum from rough grazing to arable monoculture that this study has not attempted to differentiate. **A further caveat on T3-08 itself, added 20 September 2026 (T3-12): the ≥20ha threshold used to identify "large parcels" in that analysis is smaller — in some cases much smaller — than real AI data-centre campuses actually need (Hunterston, this study's own live Scottish example, sits on 88.16ha). The 81-87% predicted southern share for large parcels may therefore rest on parcels that are "large" only relative to a derelict-land inventory, not large enough for the use this study is actually asking about; a re-cut at a higher, more realistic threshold has not yet been done.** **A further, opposite-direction data point (T3-13, 20 September 2026): the farmland counterfactual, unlike the brownfield one, does not narrow the residual gap — it widens it, since farmland is more available north of B4, not south, making the pipeline's farmland-sited concentration in the south harder, not easier, to explain by land availability alone.** Set against that, an unverified secondary lead points the other way: a lost north-of-B4 AI Growth Zone bid and trade-press "AI-ready" commentary (pointing toward the primary hypothesis), and SSEN Transmission's own substation inventory shows dense pre-existing grid infrastructure across the north (narrowing, not resolving, a grid-availability counter-explanation).

### What survives scrutiny without relying on the crude proxy: five developers, five logics, one geography — plus a sixth line of evidence from the grid itself

**Apatura** (10 of 23 projects, once Hermiston and the last four checked projects are counted) publicly argues for siting "north of the B6 Boundary" to relieve curtailment. B6 is Scotland's southern border — trivially satisfied by any Scottish site. None of Apatura's ten projects reach the stricter, more meaningful "north of B4" standard its own argument implies — including Plains/Drumshangie's former-opencast-mine brownfield reuse and Coldstream's larger, now-corrected 500MW figure.

**ILI Group** (3 projects — Cato, Rufus, Aurelius, ~1,540MW) also sites entirely south of B4, but doesn't even claim grid-constraint benefit. It frames its three-site network as a **"Green Digital Corridor linking East, Central, and West Scotland"** — jobs and regional economic distribution, not curtailment relief.

**CX Power** (Chapelcross, 1GW — the single largest identified project) also sites south of B4/B6 boundary geography, with a *third* distinct public rationale: co-locating "high-demand digital infrastructure with large-scale clean energy supply" specifically to "reduce transmission losses and improve system resilience" — framed as national decarbonisation and energy-security policy. This is close to genuine system-relevant language (transmission-loss reduction is a real grid concept), yet Chapelcross — despite reusing a former nuclear site's substation, real and valuable infrastructure reuse — still sits on the low-constraint-value side of B4.

**Eneus Energy** (Hunterston, North Ayrshire) also sites south of B4, with a *fourth* rationale: an existing energy/industrial-hub location (port, rail, high-voltage transmission connection), on a site that happens to be a former nuclear station but without the explicit substation-reuse claim CX Power makes for Chapelcross. As T3-12 found, this site is on Grade 3 agricultural (greenfield), not brownfield, land — the pipeline's one clear example of genuine hyperscale-campus scale (88.16ha) did not, in fact, use brownfield land.

**SDCL** (Cockenzie, East Lothian, former coal power station) sites south of B4 too, with a *fifth* rationale — and the sharpest test case in the whole dataset: its own public materials explicitly cite "existing grid infrastructure, brownfield status **and proximity to significant renewable energy resources**." Cockenzie provides the strongest *observed* counterexample in the pipeline, because its developer explicitly cites proximity to renewable resources as a siting rationale — and even that project remains south of B4. This is an observation about the one case that comes closest to testing the hypothesis directly, not a pre-registered falsification test; the dataset was not built by first specifying what would count as disconfirming evidence, so this should be read as the strongest data point found rather than a hypothesis that survived a designed test.

**A sixth, independent line of evidence — from the grid data, not from developer rationale:** other sectors (green hydrogen, battery storage) ARE queued for large flexible demand connections north of B4, including at New Deer itself (see Layer 2, and Tier 2 above).

**Combined: five developers/projects (65% of Layer-1 by count, ~80% by aggregate stated capacity) plus one grid-data-derived fact, all pointing the same way, now against a fully verified 23-project pipeline.** Five different public rationales (grid-benefit claimed but not met on the stricter reading; no grid claim at all; transmission-loss reduction; industrial-hub reuse; explicit general renewables proximity), plus a fourth site-reuse variant found in this pass (Drumshangie's former-opencast-mine brownfield, distinct from the power-station-reuse pattern), converging on the same geography, reinforced by direct evidence that an alternative connection point was available and being used by others, is a materially stronger basis than a single developer's strategy or an untested assumption about grid availability. It also sharpens the finding's precise shape: several forms of legitimate infrastructure logic (transmission-loss reduction, regeneration-land availability, employment distribution, general renewables adjacency, brownfield reuse of every kind) are being satisfied by Central Belt siting, while the *specific* thing this study measures — constraint-absorption exposure at B4 — is not being addressed by any of them, and not because the connection opportunity was unavailable. What this does *not* yet show is whether Central Belt siting was also the only commercially realistic option for the pipeline as a whole — T3-08's brownfield-land analysis explains most, but arguably not quite all, of the concentration for the brownfield-reuse subset, and no equivalent counterfactual exists yet for the farmland-sited majority.

### Verdict on H₀, as of today's evidence (revised, softened)

**H₀ is not confirmed, but nor is it yet displaced.** What the evidence supports is narrower and more defensible than the earlier verdict: the AI/data-centre pipeline is strongly (not just "systematically," a word that implies a completeness this dataset — real but not a full census of every possible Scottish project — cannot guarantee) concentrated south of B4, in a pattern consistent with, but not proof of, the primary hypothesis. Two things would move this from "consistent with" to "established":

1. **A genuine £/MW-year or MW-headroom score** at the key hubs (New Deer above all), which would show whether the north side actually had usable spare capacity to offer — not just an open connection queue.
2. **A complete commercial-site counterfactual (Tier 3).** The brownfield/derelict-land slice of this is now precisely quantified (T3-08): land availability alone predicts an 81-87% southern share, not the 91-96% actually observed, leaving a real but modest 5-10 percentage-point residual gap that is at the edge of what this sample size can distinguish from noise — and that quantification itself now carries a further open question about whether its ≥20ha "large parcel" threshold is actually large enough to be meaningful (T3-12). The farmland slice — where most of the pipeline actually sits — is now addressed to a first approximation too (T3-13), and it points the other way: total farmland is majority north of B4, so land availability explains *less* of the observed concentration for the farmland-sited majority than it does for the brownfield-reuse minority, not more. A genuinely current, local-authority-level version of that farmland analysis (matching T3-08's rigor rather than T3-13's first-pass sub-regional one) is still missing, and the farmland majority remains understood to be a spectrum of land-management intensity rather than one uniform category (T3-10).

What's genuinely firm, and does not depend on either of those: the Layer-1 pipeline is complete (23 of 23 verified, one capacity correction, zero new projects north of B4); five independent developers, with five different public rationales — including the one case (Cockenzie) that explicitly claims general renewables-proximity benefit — have all landed on the same side of B4; the grid's own connection data confirms that large flexible-demand connections were being sought and granted north of B4 by other sectors, at the exact substation hosting one of the three most-curtailed wind farms in the country, at the same time the AI pipeline was forming entirely on the other side; and the best available land-availability counterfactual for the brownfield-reuse minority (T3-08) shows the observed concentration there is larger than land availability alone would predict, though only by a modest margin, on a parcel-size threshold that itself now needs re-examining. For the farmland-sited majority, the equivalent counterfactual (T3-13) points the other way: farmland itself is more available north of B4, which means land availability explains *less* of that portion of the pipeline's concentration, not more — a genuine, if first-approximation, complication for H₀ specifically on the farmland side. That is a real, well-evidenced pattern with two different non-curtailment land stories sitting underneath its two halves: one (brownfield) mostly, though not completely, explained by where the land is; the other (farmland) considerably less explained by where the land is, once quality rather than raw quantity is accounted for. Whether either residual represents a genuine *mismatch* against what was achievable, rather than noise, an unmeasured factor, or (for farmland) something about accessibility and buildability this first-pass analysis can't see, is the open question this study has narrowed considerably but not closed.

### Secondary question: does the pipeline distinguish training from inference?

No project in the Layer-1 table states a workload type, and this is directly consequential, not a minor gap: the entire economic case for siting near curtailed northern wind rests on training's latency-insensitivity (EC-05) — inference needs to be near population centres for an independent, entirely legitimate reason that has nothing to do with land, grid, or curtailment. If the pipeline is mostly inference, its southern concentration needs no curtailment-related explanation at all; if it's mostly training, the absence of northern siting is harder to explain away.

**Checked 20 September 2026 (see `evidence-log.md`, Thread "Workload-type signal check across the pipeline," WT-01 through WT-05), since planning documents are confirmed silent: developer marketing materials, press coverage, named tenants, and architecture/cooling clues were checked instead.** Result: mostly negative. 21 of 23 projects show no usable signal either way — generic "AI/hyperscale/HPC" developer branding that doesn't distinguish training from inference, and no named tenants anywhere in the pipeline. Two exceptions: **DataVita's Chapelhall sites** (WT-01) show a genuine, if modest, signal toward general-purpose/colocation framing — "Home of The Multi-Cloud," direct cloud on-ramps, Tier III multi-tenant colo — consistent with a legitimate non-curtailment reason for that slice's (~112.5MW+ of ~6,840MW) southern siting. **Blackdog** (WT-02) shows circumstantial siting logic consistent with training (remote former-MOD site, built around offshore wind access) but no source states this explicitly, so it should be read as plausible, not confirmed. One genuine trap surfaced and flagged: Eneus Energy's Hunterston page names Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta and Netflix only as illustrative examples of the wider AI/cloud sector, not as tenants of that specific site (WT-03) — worth being careful never to cite these as confirmed tenants.

**Net effect: this check was worth doing, but it doesn't move the study's verdict.** The workload-type question remains genuinely unresolved for the large majority of the pipeline, so it can't currently be used to strengthen or weaken the primary hypothesis — though the one usable data point (DataVita) points toward *less*, not more, of the pipeline needing a curtailment-related explanation.

3. **A proper £/MW-year system-value score** across all eight hubs, not just New Deer — blocked on the same transformer-topology and confidentiality questions as (2), at each site.
